Context · Problem framing

A national policy launched, with no way yet to tell if it worked

In November 2023, the CDC recommended testing every infant exposed to hepatitis C with a PCR test, which detects the virus itself, at 2–6 months, instead of waiting for an antibody test at 18 months or later, once the mother's antibodies have cleared. Waiting until 18 months meant many exposed infants were never tested at all.

The team's question sounded simple: did practice actually change? My job was to make it answerable with data the team could trust.

01 · Metric definition

Defined 3 success metrics for a policy outcome

I split one vague question into three measurable ones, each with a defined metric. "Did it work" could mean more tests, earlier tests, or fairer access.

same move as turning "is the feature working?" into a success metric
ImpactDid on-time testing rise?Measure: infants with a first PCR test at 2–6 months, per 1,000 births
TimingAre infants tested earlier, not just more often?Measure: cumulative share tested by age in months
EquityDid every group benefit?Measure: on-time testing by race and ethnicity, before vs. after

02 · Data engineering in R

Built an analysis-ready cohort from 10 years of claims data

Claims data isn't organized around the question. I built the cohort in R and solved four problems along the way:

  • Split across files. Test claims and birth records lived in separate files, so I joined them on enrollee ID.
  • 10 years of yearly files. I stacked a decade of HCV test claims, millions of records, into one table.
  • Every age, not just infants. I kept only claims belonging to infants in the birth records.
  • Mothers mixed in. Birth codes also matched mothers' delivery stays. I kept records where birth year equals admission year, a rule anyone on the team can audit.
caught a silent bug. without it, every rate would have been wrong

  2. 2Pull birth recordsBirth-related hospital records
  3. 3Separate infantsBirth year = admission year removes mothers
  4. 4Join on enrollee IDLink each infant to their test claims
  5. ResultAnalysis-ready cohort800K+ infants, one row each

03 · Data quality & decision-making

Set reporting rules so incomplete data couldn't mislead

I chose to report less rather than show numbers that would mislead.

The problem

Infants born in the most recent years hadn't reached 18–24 months by the end of the data, so their antibody testing looked artificially low, a falling line that invited the wrong conclusion.

My solution

End each series where infants were too young to finish the testing window, instead of reporting every year with an easy-to-miss caveat. Fewer data points, but every one can be trusted.

skill shown: knowing when data isn't ready to report

Summer internship, 10 weeks. I owned discovery, requirements, and the MVP definition; engineering owned implementation.

Leadership asked for an AI summary for field sales, with one firm rule: the AI could inform reps but never steer them. I turned that one-line ask into a grounded, compliance-safe MVP and roadmap that leadership approved and engineering committed to build.

The insight that shaped everything

Reps can keep track of one doctor. Nobody can keep track of a hospital.

"Before I visit a hospital, I want to know what my colleagues have already heard there."

Field rep, paraphrased from interviews

Before visiting a healthcare organization, a rep needs to know what every colleague has learned there, across every doctor they've met. That context was buried in piles of free-text notes no one had time to read. So I scoped the AI to summarize the whole organization, not the individual doctor.

this one line reshaped the whole MVP

The ask

Novartis field reps log every doctor visit in Veeva CRM, the sales platform built for pharma. A year earlier, the team had added free-text notes so reps could capture what dropdowns couldn't. The notes piled up faster than anyone could read them.

Leadership wanted AI summaries to help reps prepare for their next visits. My job was to make that specific enough to build, inside a company where AI use is tightly governed. In pharma terms, a doctor is an HCP and a hospital or clinic network is an HCO.

Each round of input reshaped the plan

  2. v25 field repsUse cases rewritten, scoped to the HCO, needs prioritized
  3. v3Engineering leadRetrieval redesigned to fit existing data
  4. v4UX teamSummary placed on the HCO profile page
  5. HandoffApprovedLeadership approved; engineering committed the MVP for Q4

Leadership was firm: the AI reports what happened, and the rep decides what to do. Reps would only rely on a summary they could verify, so I designed around one rule: nothing appears in a summary without a source behind it.

Trust: every line can be verified

Each theme is one scannable sentence, because reps skim before a visit. A drop-down reveals supporting excerpts from past notes, and each excerpt links to the full original note with the relevant passage highlighted. Every summary ends with the list of calls it drew on, with each call's date and HCP, so reps know exactly what it covers.

Accuracy: six guardrails against hallucination

I specified six requirements for engineering. Each one closes a different way an AI summary can go wrong.

no source, no theme
GuardrailWhat it doesRisk it closes
Retrieval-grounded generationThe model sees only the cleared notes for that HCO and lookback window, nothing elseOutside knowledge or other accounts leaking in
Citation-required outputEvery theme carries the IDs of its source notes; themes without citations are discardedInvented themes
Verbatim evidenceExcerpts are copied from the notes, not generated, and a check confirms each one exists in its sourceFabricated quotes
Structured outputA fixed format of theme, evidence, and note IDs instead of free proseSummaries drifting into advice
Low temperatureMinimal creative variation in generationInconsistent or embellished summaries
Minimum-evidence thresholdFewer than three notes returns "not enough history" instead of themesGuessing from thin data

Safety: compliance on both ends

Notes already passed through a compliance loop: an AI screens each submitted note for risk, flagged notes go to a human reviewer, and rejected notes are dropped. I fed the summary only notes that had cleared that loop, joined with call data from Veeva, into the model the product already used. Every generated summary then runs through its own compliance check before a rep sees it. A summary can never bring back content compliance already removed.

System: fast, consistent, affordable

Summarization strategy. Context length was not a constraint, even at a one-year lookback, so the summary could cover whatever window a rep chose.

Freshness and speed. The tech lead confirmed generation was fast, so summaries are created when a rep first opens an HCO page, then stored and reused. Reps see the same summary each time they return, which builds trust, and the product saves tokens and load time.

Model choice. The summary runs on the model the product already used, rather than introducing a new one.

Quality: how I'd evaluate it

The internship ended before evaluation could run, so I scoped an evaluation plan for the team to use before launch. It tests the summary against leadership's core rule as well as for accuracy.

CheckQuestion it answersHow
FaithfulnessIs every theme supported by the notes it cites?Reviewers check sampled summaries line by line against their source notes
CoverageDid the summary miss anything important?Reviewers read all notes for sampled HCOs and compare them with the themes
NeutralityDoes it report without advising?Reviewers flag any recommendation or opinion language
Citation integrityDo cited note IDs and excerpts match real notes?Logged automatically from the pipeline's quote check
Edge casesDoes it hold up on thin or long histories?A test set with fewer than three notes, one-year windows, and busy vs. quiet brands
Real-world valueDoes it actually help reps?Pilot metrics: prep time, source-open rate, adoption

Faithfulness and neutrality are the launch gates. A summary that invents a theme or offers advice breaks the one rule leadership set, so it would block launch regardless of how useful reps found it.

Proposed plan; not yet run.

Roadmap and handoff

The roadmap sequences features by dependency, not just by rank: both later phases reuse the summary the MVP builds.

At handoff, engineering received a prioritized backlog for the MVP and later phases, Apex user stories with acceptance criteria, a technical data flow, a Figma prototype of the summary on the HCO profile, and the three-phase roadmap with success metrics.

As a field rep preparing to visit a healthcare organization, I want a summary of recurring themes from my colleagues' recent notes there, so that I walk in knowing what has already been discussed.

  • Given I'm on an HCO profile, when I open the summary, then it covers cleared notes from every HCP in that HCO.
  • Given I choose a lookback window, then only notes within that window are summarized.
  • Given a theme is shown, when I expand it, then I see its supporting excerpts, each linking to the original note with the passage highlighted.
  • Given fewer than three notes exist in the window, then the summary says there isn't enough history instead of generating themes.
  • Given any summary, then it lists the calls it drew on, with each call's date and HCP.

Outcome

Leadership approved the plan and adopted it as proposed, and engineering committed to building the MVP in Q4. The product lead estimates about $30M in benefit from time saved and better-quality interactions, including roughly 30% less time spent preparing for calls.

How we'll know it worked: call-prep time saved, measured in a pilot; the share of reps who open the summary before HCO visits; how often they open sources to verify a theme; and which lookback window they choose. Summary quality gets checked by human review against the source notes. The guardrail is that reps keep writing notes at the same rate, because summaries are only as good as the notes behind them.
